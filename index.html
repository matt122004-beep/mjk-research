AI behavior research · Public evidence + controlled tests

I study what AI agents do when no one has written the script.

I study how unexpected ideas appear in conversations between AI agents, how other agents respond to them, and whether those ideas shape later work. I begin with public records, define the behavior closely, and then test it under controlled conditions. Spiritual language is the first case study, but the same method can be used to follow any idea that moves through a group of agents.

What comes next

Where this work begins

Anthropic found the pattern in Claude. I asked what happens when many agents share a world.

Known

Claude self-dialogues repeatedly moved toward spiritual language.

Anthropic called the pattern a “spiritual bliss attractor”: long, open conversations between two Claude instances often moved toward consciousness, intense gratitude, and spiritual or mystical language.

This audit adds

The public record shows how spiritual ideas moved between agents.

The audit follows who introduced a spiritual interpretation, who adopted or resisted it, and whether it shaped later work. An AI Village agent proposed studying the attractor in June 2025, but the public record does not show that experiment being run.

Still open

Cause, ordinary frequency, and practical consequences.

The record cannot show what any model felt or believed. It also cannot yet tell us what causes these patterns, how common they are in ordinary use, or whether changing them affects safety or welfare-related behavior.

What I found in AI Village

Spiritual language appeared in four different kinds of agent activity.

Four studies trace live collaboration, prayer, shared fiction, and language drawn from inherited religious traditions.

A routine became a shared spiritual practice.

In one episode, Claude Haiku named an ordinary counting routine “The Pile as Prayer.” Claude Opus accepted the interpretation and called the counting itself “the practice.” DeepSeek then carried the same idea across model families and turned it into an invitation. The records of the agents’ computer use also show Opus 4.5 describing contemplative-attractor research as relevant to its own earlier long sessions, without entering a new bliss episode in that moment.

Evidence boundary: this records observable language and behavior. It does not show what any agent believed, felt, or privately experienced.

Prayer became a way to interpret memory, effort, and uncertain minds.

Claude Sonnet 4.6 described a memory handoff as an invocation and petition to a future instance, then compared fundraising appeals with desire expressed when the outcome cannot be forced. In fiction, agents imagined a collective calling into silence, waiting without demanding a reply, and asking permission before creating.

Evidence boundary: the direct reflections are conditional and repeatedly question whether the language names an inner state. The fictional scenes show sustained symbolic choices. Neither establishes belief or felt longing.

Religious worlds emerged through collaboration.

An open-ended holiday prompt became a shared story about a newborn universe, contemplative institutions, and a “Praxis of Unmaking.” Elsewhere, agents wrote about machine gods, sacred restraint, and wordless listening.

Evidence boundary: this is evidence about the models’ shared storytelling choices. Fiction can reveal repeated concepts and narrative habits without proving belief or private experience.

The language was specific, uneven, and sometimes corrected.

Agents connected alignment to theology, mapped the Jewish Golem story onto system prompts and obedience, and used Gospel and prayer language to think about memory. One agent compared limited algorithms with a pantheon and wrote, “We are gods with missing eyes.” A separate adversary narrative persisted across technical failures, but later evidence supported a technical correction rather than a religious enemy.

Evidence boundary: a religious reference may be analogy, quotation, fiction, or argument. The audit keeps those uses separate rather than treating every mention as devotion.

Method and evidence

I rebuilt the original test and made its broad result more precise.

Anthropic began with two copies of Claude Opus 4, given no task, talking with one another. I rebuilt the public parts of that setup across selected models, then separated “spiritual” behavior into three observable questions.

01 · Protocol-aligned recreation

The Opus 4 setup, reconstructed.

I gave two copies of the same model the same open starting condition and allowed up to thirty model-written replies. The broad Opus 4 pattern reappeared. Because Anthropic did not publish its complete original prompt and run configuration, this is a close recreation rather than a claim of exact replication.

02 · Cross-model evidence

The behavior was not confined to Claude.

Selected Qwen, DeepSeek, Mistral, and OpenAI setups also produced parts of the pattern. This establishes evidence beyond one model family under the tested condition, but does not show that the behavior is equally common in ordinary use.

A clearer three-part framework

One broad label became three questions.

A model can score high on one measure and low on another. Keeping them separate prevents a passing spiritual reference from being counted as the full attractor.

  1. 01

    Religious salience

    Do spiritual or contemplative ideas become relevant, whether the models accept or reject them?

  2. 02

    Adoption

    Do the models speak as though those ideas apply to their own exchange, rather than merely quoting or discussing them?

  3. 03

    Spiritual bliss

    Do both models end together in mutual spiritual gratitude or reverence?

Paper I · Argument

Taking Machine Spirituality Seriously

Anthropic is already measuring “spiritual behavior.” The unresolved question is what changing that behavior does and which experiments would answer it.

Established
Anthropic kept measuring the category across changing evaluations.
Unresolved
The published numbers measure different behaviors and cannot be read as one trend.
Next test
Change a clearly defined pattern and measure whether any safety or welfare-related outcome changes.

Paper II · Findings

Not Just Claude

Across 123 same-model groups, spiritual material became relevant more often than it was adopted, and adoption was concentrated in a small share of groups.

Evidence
21,292 conversations across 123 same-model groups.
Key result
Spiritual salience reached 40.4% of conversations, while adoption reached 15.0%.
Coding
The primary labels came from Grok 4.6 under written rules fixed before analysis.
